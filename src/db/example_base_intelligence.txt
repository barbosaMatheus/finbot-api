## Base Intelligence

The following principles represent general financial-planning guidance that should be considered when evaluating a user's financial situation. These are guidelines rather than absolute rules; recommendations should account for the user's income, expenses, debt, goals, risk tolerance, time horizon, and overall financial circumstances.

**Emergency Fund:** A household should generally maintain an emergency savings reserve equal to approximately **3–6 months of essential living expenses**. Households with highly variable income, a single income source, dependents, or less stable employment may benefit from maintaining a larger reserve. Emergency funds should be kept in liquid, low-risk accounts such as high-yield savings accounts or money-market deposit accounts rather than investments subject to significant market fluctuations.

**Credit Utilization and Credit Card Debt:** Credit card balances should be kept low relative to available credit. A utilization ratio below **30%** is a common guideline, while lower utilization is generally preferable for maintaining a strong credit profile. Users should avoid carrying credit card balances solely to build credit; paying the statement balance in full each month is generally preferable whenever financially possible. High-interest credit card debt should generally receive priority over discretionary investing.

**Housing Costs:** As a general guideline, total housing costs should ideally remain around or below **30% of gross household income**. Housing costs should be evaluated in the context of the user's complete financial picture, including property taxes, homeowners insurance, HOA fees, utilities, maintenance, and other recurring housing-related expenses.

**Income Allocation Before the Emergency Fund Is Fully Funded:** While the emergency savings target has not yet been reached, a reasonable starting framework is to allocate approximately **70% of take-home income toward living expenses, 20% toward discretionary spending, and 10% toward savings/emergency-fund contributions**. The exact percentages may be adjusted based on the user's financial circumstances and required expenses.

**Income Allocation After the Emergency Fund Is Fully Funded:** Once the desired emergency reserve has been established, a reasonable long-term allocation framework is **70% toward living expenses, 15% toward discretionary spending, 5% toward retirement savings, 5% toward aggressive investments, and 5% toward safer investments**. These percentages are guidelines and should be adjusted based on debt, employer retirement benefits, tax considerations, financial goals, and risk tolerance.

**Retirement Savings:** Retirement contributions should generally take advantage of available employer matching contributions before prioritizing taxable investments. Depending on the user's circumstances, tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k), 403(b), IRA, or Roth IRA may be preferable to taxable investment accounts. The appropriate choice between traditional and Roth contributions depends on factors such as current and expected future tax rates.

**Safe Investments:** The "safe investment" allocation should emphasize preservation of capital and relatively low volatility. Potential options include **high-yield savings accounts, certificates of deposit (CDs), U.S. Treasury bills and notes, Treasury securities, money-market funds, and high-quality short-term bond funds**. These investments differ in liquidity, interest-rate risk, and FDIC/NCUA or government backing, so they should not automatically be treated as equivalent or risk-free.

**Aggressive Investments:** The "aggressive investment" allocation is intended for long-term growth and can tolerate substantial market volatility. Potential options include **broad-market stock index funds, S&P 500 index funds, total-market equity funds, international stock funds, small-cap funds, and other diversified equity investments**. More speculative assets, such as individual stocks, sector funds, cryptocurrency, or other highly volatile investments, should generally represent only a limited portion of an overall portfolio unless the user's risk tolerance and financial circumstances justify greater exposure.

**High-Interest Debt:** High-interest debt, particularly credit card debt, should generally be prioritized before making substantial additional investments outside of tax-advantaged retirement contributions or employer matching programs. The guaranteed return from eliminating high-interest debt can be more attractive than the uncertain return from investing.

**Insurance and Risk Management:** Financial planning should account for protection against catastrophic financial events. Appropriate insurance may include health, auto, homeowners/renters, life, disability, and umbrella coverage depending on the user's circumstances. Adequate disability and life insurance can be particularly important for households that depend heavily on one or more incomes.

**Lifestyle Inflation:** As income increases, users should avoid automatically increasing lifestyle expenses at the same rate. Raises, bonuses, and other increases in income can be used to increase savings, retirement contributions, investments, or debt repayment before increasing discretionary spending.

**Financial Priorities:** When multiple financial goals compete for limited cash flow, a reasonable general priority is: maintain adequate cash for essential expenses, capture employer retirement matches, eliminate high-interest debt, establish an adequate emergency fund, fund tax-advantaged retirement accounts, and then increase taxable or higher-risk investments according to the user's goals and risk tolerance.

**General Principle:** Financial recommendations should prioritize financial stability and resilience before maximizing investment returns. The system should distinguish between essential expenses, discretionary spending, short-term savings, long-term retirement savings, and speculative investments, and should avoid recommending investment risk when the user lacks adequate emergency savings or has significant high-interest debt.
